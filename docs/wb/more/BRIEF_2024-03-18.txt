More than 48% of urban Tamil Nadu has previous experienced issues with respect to water
availability and unsafe sanitation access contributes to high climate-related risks. This is a result
of growing demand due to urbanization, poorly managed water systems and climate-induced seasonal
extremes. The severe northeast monsoons of 2015 resulted in a record-breaking flood in Tamil Nadu,
which was followed by the worst drought in 140 years the state faced in 2016. During the summer of
2019, four major reservoirs experienced complete depletion, leading to a “Day Zero” water crisis in the
capital city, Chennai. 3 Out of the 1,943 tanks identified by the Ministry of Water Resources, a significant
number have been revealed as sites for municipal waste disposal. The fact that WSS services in TN’s
ULBs suffer from limited coverage and quality, as well as high operational and financing
inefficiencies exacerbate climate-induced impacts (such as floods and droughts) to the
population. In most ULBs, residents receive water for only one to three hours per day, with the
frequency varying from daily to once in every five days. Bulk water supply levels are at 65 liters per
capita per day (lpcd) compared to the national guideline of 135 lpcd. Thus, the population often depend
on informal water supply (such as water trucks, private vendors or unregulated groundwater) to meet
their needs, but these sources tend to become unavailable and unsafe in climate extreme events. The
quality of service is further strained by uncontrolled non-revenue water (NRW), which leads to wastage
of fresh water sources. Access to sanitation services is poor as well and raises public health
concerns relating to water source contamination from flood and storm waters. For the one-third
of ULBs with sewerage networks, service coverage is less than 40 percent. Many of the existing sewage
treatment plants (STPs) suffer from poor operation and maintenance (O&M). A systematic planning
through a combination of sewerage and non-sewerage systems to ensure a citywide access to safely
managed sanitation is missing. As such, a large part of the population still relies on unsafely managed
sanitation, which exposes them to health risks in a flooding event. Women and children also suffer the
most from the lack of improved sanitation, which causes diseases and increases the need for health
care support, as well as absence from school.
Energy Efficiency is a non-regret, climate-friendly, source of savings to ULBs. The Alliance to
Save Energy’s Watergy program launched in 2007 by TNUIFSL has demonstrated that, by optimizing
energy use, municipalities can achieve significant energy and monetary savings, at least 20 percent in
water supply systems alone. By updating municipal infrastructure, improved utility services can be
provided to the population and industries while simultaneously reducing pollution, GHG emissions and
costs. Some of the participating ULBs like Vellore, Cuddalore, Pudukottai and Dindigul were also a
part of the municipal energy efficiency project by TNUIFSL. However, increasing urban population
and extreme temperatures will lead to further increase in energy consumptions that attribute to rise in
GHG emissions 4.
ULB’s face institutional capacity and policy challenges. Insights from consultations with ULBs
revealed that tackling climate change is presently limited to adaptation measures like preparedness
for disaster management. The latest government order with sanctioned positions for ULBs in Tamil
Nadu does not contain dedicated personnel for climate change and resilience, thus limiting planning,
financing and rolling out robust climate actions. Furthermore, ULBs currently lack mechanisms to
incorporate climate and disaster risks in their investment identification and prioritization procedures,
and there is a lack of contingency planning for impending risk of climate hazards. Thus, there is a
strong case for enhancing the institutional capacities and increasing investments for incorporating
climate resilience in ULBs and the proposed urban WSS infrastructure systems.

further reduction in emissions due progressive city-wide access to safely managed sanitation

(energy efficiency, parks, NRW, CWIS) have not been quantified in the GHG calculations.

Paris Alignment
The operation is aligned with the goals of the Paris Agreement focused on mitigation, adaptation, and
resilience.
Consistency with the country’s NDC, LTS, and NAP A review of the wastewater management
projects under the Program shows a strong potential to reduce GHG emissions as the projects will
lead to reducing methane emissions from untreated wastewater through scientific treatment and
disposal of the same (see GHG accounting paragraph). Moreover, low carbon technological design
and interventions and the City Climate Action Plan (CCAP) (such as energy efficiency, increased
treatment of the wastewater, CWIS, green areas) will ensure future WSS projects are set on a low
carbon trajectory, contributing to the overall climate goals of the state (in line with INDCs). To ensure
that all Program activities are aligned with India’s NDCs, India’s Long-Term Low-Carbon Development
Strategy (2022), and National Action Plan on Climate Change (NAPCC 2021), specific DLRs have
been proposed (DLRs 3 and 8, and parts of DLR 6 and 7) which include clear activities and results
towards climate adaptation and mitigation.
Climate Baseline assessment: climate vulnerability situation of program ULBs was described earlier
in this document. On the institutional responses side, it was identified that tackling climate change is
limited preparedness for disaster management. ULBs in Tamil Nadu does not contain dedicated
personnel for climate change and resilience, thus limiting planning, financing, and rolling out robust
climate actions. Furthermore, ULBs currently lack mechanisms to incorporate climate and disaster
risks in their investment identification and prioritization procedures, and there is a lack of contingency
planning for impending risk of climate hazards.
Climate resilience interventions are integrated under both the result areas under the Program.
While RA1 focuses on strengthening urban management and climate resilient planning at ULB level,
RA2 prioritizes improving accessibility, climate resilience and financial sustainability of urban services.
On one hand, RA1 prioritizes the adoption of CCAP for each ULB, which includes climate risk
vulnerability assessment, GHG inventories, adaptation and mitigation measures based on their
vulnerabilities, and identifying alternative water sources to ensure water security. On the other hand,
RA2 incentivizes the ULBs to mainstream climate resilience aspects in investment design and O&M of
proposed WSS infrastructure, including adopting a detailed climate risk screening checklist and
incorporating resilient measures in the design of their WSS projects. The Program incentivizes the
development and approval of the water security and emergency plan for WSS services (RA1), which
are intended to better prepare the service providers to maintain WSS services (RA2) in case of a
climate-related emergency. The use of a program for results instrument (PforR) supports carrying out
climate related activities in different levels: physical designs, O&M of assets, service delivery,
planning, institutional strengthening.
Assessment and reduction of mitigation risks: Program has a low risk of preventing the country’s
transition to low-carbon development pathways, since (i) all the activities financed are either universally
aligned or are expected to lead to low GHG emission impacts; (ii) a mechanism for tracking and lowering
GHG emissions for activities with moderate GHG emissions intensity have been assessed and
integrated in the Program design; (iii) activities supported by the Program and the economic impacts of
the PforR are not expected to be at risk from low-carbon transition. The operation is not at material risk
of having a negative impact on the country’s low-GHG emissions development pathways. All of the
activities financed under the Program are on the universally aligned list, including the expansion and
rehabilitation of water supply systems 6, NRW management, wastewater management systems (from
collection to effluents treatment), conducting of energy audits and improving of energy efficiency, and
constructing of green spaces.
The proposed wastewater projects represent the largest portion of program investments. In this sector,
Program activities support a full sanitation chain, from household connections all the way through
treating and discharging effluents at adequate standards. These activities enable a robust reduction in
GHG emissions, as described earlier in this document. For the WSS infrastructure assets financed
under the Program, energy consumption from pumping stations, motors, and treatment plants are the
potential sources of GHG emissions. To ensure that all Program activities are aligned with India’s NDCs,
India’s Long-Term Low-Carbon Development Strategy (2022), and NAPCC 2021, specific targets have
been proposed under RAs 1 and 2. The Program will incentivize the adoption of robust CCAP by each
ULB, which includes preparation of CWIS plans to improve sanitation facilities and city-wide safe
disposal of sewage, leading to a progressive (medium-to-long term) reduction of GHG emissions.
Increasing green space will help mitigate heat island effects, increase rainfall infiltration, and contribute
to carbon sequestration. The ULBs are incentivized to carry out energy audits to closely monitor their
energy consumption and achieve systematic improvement in energy efficiency for their municipal
assets. The Program will also ensure progressive reduction in emissions through incentivizing the
optimization of existing assets and efficient operation of WSS assets. The proposed wastewater
management subprojects would lead to an increase in sewerage connections and treatment of
wastewater, directly leading to a net reduction in lifetime emissions of 6,786,713 tCO2-eq.

Assessment and reduction of adaptation risks. The ULBs in Program areas have a high exposure
to climate change risks, particularly water scarcity, floods, and extreme heat. The Program’s climate
risk reduction measures would help the 21 participating ULBs to adapt to them. RA1 prioritizes the
adoption of CCAPs for the ULBs based on assessment of climate vulnerability and GHG inventories,
and implementation of adaptation and mitigation measures including identifying alternative water
sources to ensure water security, promoting city-wide access to improved sanitation (including
adequate treatment) and ensuring water supply and sanitation infrastructure are resilient to flooding
and other disasters. RA2 incentivizes the ULBs to mainstream climate resilience aspects in investment
design, planning, and operation of WSS services. A climate risk screening checklist and resilience
design guideline have been developed by TNUIFSL outlining climate risk aspects and resilience
measures pertaining to WSS systems, and the ULBs are required to incorporate these measures in the
design of their WSS projects. This will help pre-empt and prevent potential impacts to WSS systems
from climate-exacerbated floods and droughts, for example, restricting potential contamination of raw
water in service reservoirs due to flooding, reducing disruption in road connectivity, and ensuring access
to pump houses during flooding. In the case of wastewater management systems, enhanced resilience
to floods will lead to lower operational disruptions caused by power fluctuations causing obstructions in
pumping, which may lead to sewage overflows and flooding of STPs, while drought preparedness will
limit the impact of silting of sewers due to low flows. For each type of risk, the guideline recommends
specific measures for the design and O&M stages.
Overall, core Program activities such as improvement in WSS coverage, performance standards, and
an overall effective institutional framework will enhance operational efficiency and sustainability of
services, thus helping enhance resilience to droughts, floods, and extreme temperatures. The IPF
component will also include training and capacity building for the ULBs in the management of WSS
services during climate emergencies. In addition, through DLI 8, ULBs are encouraged to adopt a
targeted approach to achieve systematic improvement in energy efficiency for municipal assets. ULBs
will also achieve increase in their carbon sink, through developments of parks which will lead to increase
in green cover. Apart from carbon sequestration benefits, this will also have significant adaptation
benefits by preventing the formation of urban heat islands. Disbursement linked to these indicators,
aims to serve as necessary incentive for ULBs to prioritize improved climate performance through
progressive implementation of specific and relevant climate actions. Thus, risks from climate hazards
have been reduced to an acceptable level and are not likely to have material impact on the operation
and its development objective.
